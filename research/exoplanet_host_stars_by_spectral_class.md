# Exoplanet Discoveries by Stellar Spectral Classification

## Overview
Of the more than $6,500$ confirmed exoplanets discovered to date, planetary systems have been verified around **6 of the 7 main-sequence stellar spectral classes ($\text{B, A, F, G, K, and M}$)** on the Morgan–Keenan scale, as well as around evolved giant stars, brown dwarfs, and stellar remnants (pulsars and white dwarfs). 

The table below outlines the distribution of known exoplanets across the main-sequence spectral classes, why observational biases and stellar physics affect each class, and notable landmark discoveries.

---

## Main-Sequence Host Stars (The Morgan–Keenan Scale)

| Spectral Class | Color & Effective Temp ($\text{K}$) | Approximate Share of Known Exoplanets | Observational & Physical Factors (Why We Find or Don't Find Planets Here) | Notable Historical Systems |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **O-type** | Blue ($28,000\text{–}50,000\text{ K}$) | **$0\%$** *(None on main sequence)* | Extreme ultraviolet radiation and stellar winds strip away protoplanetary disks via photoevaporation in less than $1\text{ million}$ years. These stars also go supernova within a few million years. | *None* (Though evolved hot subdwarfs like *NY Virginis* have planetary candidates, no main-sequence O-star hosts a confirmed planet). |
| **B-type** | Blue-White ($10,000\text{–}28,000\text{ K}$) | **$< 0.2\%$** *(Very Rare)* | Difficult to detect via radial velocity (very few spectral absorption lines) or transits (stars are massive and blindingly bright). However, direct imaging has caught massive gas giants in wide orbits. | **b Centauri (AB) b** (2021) — a $10\text{-}M_{\text{J}}$ planet orbiting a massive B-type binary at $550\text{ AU}$; **Kappa Andromedae b** (2012). |
| **A-type** | White ($7,500\text{–}10,000\text{ K}$) | **$\sim 1\text{–}2\%$** | Young A-type stars often retain prominent debris disks. Because they rotate rapidly and pulsate, they are prime targets for **direct imaging** rather than Doppler radial velocity surveys. | **HR 8799** (2008) — first multi-planet system directly imaged; **Fomalhaut**; **Beta Pictoris b**. |
| **F-type** | Yellow-White ($6,000\text{–}7,500\text{ K}$) | **$\sim 10\text{–}12\%$** | Hotter and brighter than the Sun with wider habitable zones, making them strong targets for both radial velocity and transit surveys (such as *Kepler* and *TESS*). | **Upsilon Andromedae** (1999) — first multi-planet system discovered around a main-sequence star; **HD 209458 b** (first transiting exoplanet). |
| **G-type** *(Solar twins)* | Yellow ($5,000\text{–}6,000\text{ K}$) | **$\sim 45\text{–}50\%$** | Heavily targeted by the *Kepler* mission and early Doppler surveys looking for Sun-like analogs. Slow rotation and rich heavy-element absorption lines make Doppler shifts easier to measure. | **51 Pegasi b** (1995) — first confirmed planet around a main-sequence star; **Kepler-452b**; **HD 10180**. |
| **K-type** *(Orange dwarfs)* | Orange ($3,500\text{–}5,000\text{ K}$) | **$\sim 20\text{–}25\%$** | Considered the **"Goldilocks" stars** for astrobiology: dimmer and smaller than G-stars (making transits deeper), stable for $15\text{–}45\text{ billion}$ years, and less prone to violent flares than M-dwarfs. | **Gamma Cephei Ab** (tentatively detected 1988, confirmed 2002); **Epsilon Eridani b**; **Kepler-442b**. |
| **M-type** *(Red dwarfs)* | Red ($2,500\text{–}3,500\text{ K}$) | **$\sim 15\text{–}20\%$** *(Fastest growing)* | Make up $\sim 75\%$ of all stars in the Milky Way. Their small size and low mass mean even Earth-sized planets induce strong gravitational wobbles and deep transit light dips. | **Gliese 876 b** (1998, first M-dwarf planet); **Proxima Centauri b** (2016, closest exoplanet); **TRAPPIST-1** ($7$ rocky worlds). |

## Key Takeaways on the Percentages
* **Observational Bias vs. True Abundance:** While **G-type stars** currently account for nearly half of all *known* exoplanets, this is largely due to selection bias—astronomers specifically pointed the *Kepler* Space Telescope at Sun-like stars. 
* **Actual Galactic Distribution:** In reality, **M-type (red dwarf)** and **K-type (orange dwarf)** stars host the vast majority of planets in the Milky Way simply because they make up over $85\%$ of the galaxy's stellar population.
* **Non-Main-Sequence Hosts ($\sim 1\text{–}2\%$ combined):** The remaining fraction of discovered exoplanets orbit **evolved giant/subgiant stars** (such as *Aldebaran b* and *Iota Draconis b*), **brown dwarfs** (such as *2M1207b*), and **stellar remnants** including pulsars (*PSR B1257+12*) and white dwarfs (*WD 1856+534 b*).
