# Milky Way Stellar Population by Spectral Classification

## Overview

In astronomy, the **Initial Mass Function (IMF)** dictates that nature forms low-mass stars in vastly greater numbers than high-mass stars. Furthermore, because massive stars burn through their nuclear fuel in just a few million years while low-mass stars live for trillions of years, the current population of our galaxy is overwhelmingly dominated by small, cool, dim stars.

The tables below provide the breakdown of the Milky Way's stellar population—first across the **Main Sequence (Harvard/Morgan–Keenan spectral types)**, and second across **broader stellar and sub-stellar object classes** (including remnants and brown dwarfs).

## 2. All Stellar & Sub-Stellar Object Classes (Complete Galactic Census)

When you look beyond active main-sequence stars to include evolved giants, dead stellar remnants, and "failed stars" (brown dwarfs), the total census of stellar-scale objects in our galaxy breaks down as follows:

| Object Category | Luminosity / Spectral Class | Share of Total Stellar/Sub-Stellar Census | Physical Description & Origin |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Main-Sequence Stars** | Luminosity Class $\text{V}$ ($\text{O–M}$) | $\sim 75\text{–}80\%$ | Active stars fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores. Over three-quarters of these are M-type red dwarfs. |
| **Brown Dwarfs** *(Sub-stellar)* | Spectral Classes $\text{L, T, and Y}$ | $\sim 15\text{–}20\%$ | Objects between $13$ and $80$ Jupiter masses ($0.013\text{–}0.08\,M_\odot$). Too low in mass to sustain core hydrogen fusion, though they briefly fuse deuterium. |
| **White Dwarfs** *(Remnants)* | Luminosity Class $\text{VII}$ / $\text{D}$ ($\text{DA, DB}$, etc.) | $\sim 4\text{–}5\%$ | Earth-sized, dense carbon-oxygen cores left behind after $\text{A, F, G}$, and some $\text{K}$ stars shed their outer layers. Accounts for $\sim 97\%$ of all dead stars. |
| **Evolved Giants & Supergiants** | Luminosity Classes $\text{I, II, III, IV}$ | $\sim 0.4\text{–}0.5\%$ | Stars (like **Betelgeuse**, **Aldebaran**, and **Arcturus**) that have exhausted core hydrogen and swelled enormously. Rare by number because this phase is brief, though they dominate the night sky due to high brightness. |
| **Neutron Stars / Pulsars** *(Remnants)* | Collapsed cores | $\sim 0.3\text{–}0.4\%$ | City-sized, ultra-dense remnants formed from the core-collapse supernovae of massive stars ($8\text{–}20\,M_\odot$). Approximately $1\text{ billion}$ exist in the Milky Way. |
| **Stellar-Mass Black Holes** *(Remnants)* | Collapsed cores | $\sim 0.05\text{–}0.1\%$ | Formed from the collapse of the most massive $\text{O}$ and $\text{B}$ stars ($>20\,M_\odot$). An estimated $10\text{ million to }100\text{ million}$ drift through the Milky Way. |

## Key Takeaways on Galactic Demographics

* **The Night Sky Illusion (Malmquist Bias):** Even though **M-type red dwarfs** make up $\sim 76.5\%$ of all main-sequence stars, **not a single one is visible to the naked eye** from Earth because of their extreme dimness. Conversely, **O, B, A, and Giant stars** make up less than $1.2\%$ of the galaxy's stars, yet they comprise the vast majority of the bright stars we see in constellations at night.
* **The Sun is in the Top 10%:** Although our Sun ($\text{G2V}$) is often casually referred to as an "average" star, it is actually more massive and luminous than roughly **$90\%$ of all main-sequence stars** in the Milky Way.
* **Every M-Dwarf Ever Born is Still Alive:** Because the universe is $13.8\text{ billion}$ years old and K- and M-type stars live from $15\text{ billion}$ to $10\text{ trillion}$ years, every single K- and M-dwarf that has ever formed since the Big Bang is still on the main sequence today.
